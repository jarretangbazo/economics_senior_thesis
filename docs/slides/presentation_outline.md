### **SLIDE 13: Empirical Strategy** [14:00 - 15:30]

**Content**:
```
DIFFERENCE-IN-DIFFERENCES SPECIFICATION

Y_ict = β₀ + β₁(Treated_c × Post_t) + β₂X_ict + γ_c + δ_t + ε_ict

Where:
• Y_ict = Educational outcome (individual i, LGA c, cohort t)

• X_ict = Individual/household controls
• γ_c = LGA fixed effects (time-invariant characteristics)
• δ_t = Cohort fixed effects (common time trends)

β₁ = Causal effect of violence exposure

Identification Assumption: Parallel trends
```

## QUESTION & ANSWER PREPARATION [35:00 - 45:00]

### **Anticipated Questions & Answers:**

**Q1: "How do you know violence is exogenous?"**
**A**: "Great question. I don't claim violence is random—that's why I use DiD rather than simple comparison. The key assumption is parallel trends, which I test and validate. Also, my event study shows no pre-treatment effects, supporting the identification strategy. I control for LGA fixed effects to absorb time-invariant differences. That said, if there were time-varying omitted variables correlated with both violence and education, that could bias my estimates. That's a limitation, and I'm transparent about it."

**Q2: "Could migration bias your results?"**
**A**: "Excellent point. Migration could bias in either direction. If worst-affected families flee, I'd underestimate effects. If poorest families can't leave, I might overestimate. Unfortunately, my data doesn't track migration well. This is a limitation. However, my results are similar when I drop urban areas (where migration is easiest), suggesting it's not driving everything. Future work with panel data could address this better."

**Q3: "Why do girls see larger effects?"**
**A**: "My data suggests three mechanisms. First, safety concerns—Chibok showed parents that sending girls to school could be deadly. Second, when resources are scarce, households prioritize boys' education in Northern Nigeria due to cultural factors and expected returns. Third, Boko Haram explicitly targeted girls' schools. I can't definitively separate these, but the pattern is clear and consistent with literature from other contexts like Tajikistan."

**Q4: "How generalizable are your findings?"**
**A**: "The mechanisms—supply disruption, household shocks, safety concerns—likely operate in other conflict settings. The magnitude depends on context: baseline education levels, type of conflict, state capacity. My findings are consistent with studies from Rwanda, Tajikistan, Peru in direction and rough magnitude. So while I wouldn't claim exact effect sizes transfer, the qualitative patterns probably do. Nigeria is also representative of many Sub-Saharan African contexts."

**Q5: "What about alternative explanations?"**
**A**: "What did you have in mind specifically? [Listen]. Generally, I've tried to rule out alternatives through: (1) Parallel trends test rules out pre-existing differential trends. (2) Event study rules out anticipation effects. (3) Placebo tests confirm no effects when there shouldn't be. (4) Robustness checks across specifications. (5) Heterogeneity analysis confirms patterns make sense. If you're thinking of a specific alternative, I'm happy to discuss."

**Q6: "Did you consider instrumental variables?"**
**A**: "Yes, I explored IV approaches. The challenge is finding a valid instrument for violence. Rainfall shocks and ethnic diversity have been used in other papers, but both have exclusion restriction problems in my setting—they could affect education directly. I opted for DiD with careful validity tests rather than IV with questionable instruments. That said, it's a direction for future work if a better instrument emerges."

**Q7: "How does this inform World Bank programming?"**
**A**: "Very directly. First, universal education programs won't work—need to target girls specifically with safe schools, female teachers, community engagement. Second, catch-up programs are essential since effects persist. Third, poorest households need financial support to keep kids in school during shocks. Fourth, invest in school security and teacher incentives for conflict zones. I'm happy to discuss specific program design if you're interested."

**Q8: "What's your biggest concern about the analysis?"**
**A**: "I'll be honest—measurement error in the education data. It's self-reported, so there could be recall bias. Violence data might undercount low-intensity events. And I can't perfectly observe migration. These could attenuate my estimates, so my effects might be lower bounds. But multiple robustness checks give me confidence the sign and rough magnitude are right. I'm transparent about limitations in the paper."

**Q9: "Why not use more recent data?"**
**A**: "I'd love to! NEDS 2010 is the most recent comprehensive education survey with LGA identifiers that I could access. DHS has more recent data but less education detail. Ideally, I'd merge with 2018 DHS to look at long-run effects—that's actually my next step. The 1999-2010 period captures the onset and escalation of Boko Haram violence, which is analytically useful for DiD."

**Q10: "What surprised you most in the data?"**
**A**: "The magnitude of the gender gap. Literature suggested girls would be more affected, but I was struck by how much larger—nearly double. Also, the persistence—I expected some catch-up, but there's none. Children who lose schooling during conflict don't make it up later. That has huge implications for how we think about recovery and reconstruction."

**Body Language:**
- Face audience, not screen
- Move deliberately (not pacing)
- Use hand gestures to emphasize points
- Make eye contact with different parts of room
- Smile (especially at beginning and end)

**Voice:**
- Speak slowly and clearly
- Pause after key points
- Vary tone (don't monotone)
- Project to back of room
- Emphasize important findings

**Handling Nerves:**
- Deep breath before starting
- Remember: You know this better than anyone
- Slow down if nervous (natural tendency to speed up)
- It's okay to pause and think
- Have water available

**Engaging Audience:**
- Make eye contact
- "Show of hands: how many of you have worked on conflict issues?"
- "You might be wondering..."
- "This surprised me, and here's why..."
- Watch for confused faces, slow down if needed

### **During Q&A:**

**Listen Actively:**
- Let person finish question
- Repeat/rephrase if unclear
- Say "That's a great question" (even if tough)
- Take a moment to think before answering

**Answer Structure:**
- "Short answer: [direct response]"
- "Let me elaborate: [details]"
- "Does that answer your question?" (if unsure)

**If You Don't Know:**
- "That's a great question I hadn't considered"
- "I don't have data to answer that definitively, but..."
- "That would be a great extension for future work"
- Never make up an answer

**Handling Difficult Questions:**
- Stay calm and professional
- Acknowledge valid criticisms
- Explain your reasoning/constraints
- Offer to discuss offline if detailed
- Don't get defensive

**Types of Difficult Questioners:**
- **The Challenger**: Stay calm, acknowledge their point, explain your reasoning
- **The Rambler**: Politely interrupt: "Let me make sure I understand the question..."
- **The Showoff**: Give brief answer, move on quickly
- **The Confused**: Patiently clarify, use simpler language

## SLIDE DESIGN PRINCIPLES

### **Visual Hierarchy:**
- Title: 28-32pt, bold
- Body text: 18-24pt
- Captions: 14-16pt
- Never go below 14pt (unreadable from back)

### **Color Scheme:**
- Use institution's colors (if job talk)
- Or professional palette: Navy/gray/accent color
- Ensure high contrast (dark on light or vice versa)
- Test for colorblind accessibility

### **Layout:**
- Consistent template throughout
- Plenty of white space
- One main idea per slide
- Charts/graphs large enough to see from back

### **Fonts:**
- Sans-serif (Arial, Helvetica, Calibri) for body
- Consistent throughout
- Bold for emphasis, not underline
- Avoid all caps except titles

### **Graphs:**
- Large, clear labels
- Simple, clean design
- Remove unnecessary elements
- Annotate key findings
- Legend outside plot area if possible

### **Bad Slide Example:**
```
[Tiny 10pt text]
[3 different fonts]
[7 different graphs crammed together]
[Busy background]
[Impossible to read from more than 3 feet away]
```

### **Good Slide Example:**
```
CLEAR TITLE (28pt, bold)

One key finding: Violence reduces education by 0.4 years

[ONE large, clean graph showing this finding]

Takeaway: Policy must address education disruption during conflict

[Clean, simple, readable from back of room]
```
